Rainy Night Routine

The neon sign outside cast an eerie glow in the gloom of night as Jack Slade sat hunched over his desk, whiskey glass in hand and eyes lost to thought, the rain pattering against the grimy window like a thousand restless spirits vying for attention. His gaze drifted from the amber liquid within his glass to the photograph tacked up on the wall behind him—Sarah’s beaming smile, her laughter dancing in the frame.

That particular rain had become his own personal soundtrack since she vanished three long weeks ago; days turned to nights turned into a blur that refused to let up. Whiskey, not the cheap stuff now but the stuff of desperation and broken dreams, warmed him as he swirled it in his cup with a slow spin. The photograph seemed to mock him, its joy an unbridgeable chasm between then and now.

Three weeks, nearly two months posthumous. It had been seventeen years since she was taken from him on an October night very much like this one—the rain-slicked streets still fresh with promise that night, full of life yet to be washed away. Slade’s eyes stung, refusing to meet Jenkins’ gaze as he recalled those memories.

He sipped the bitter liquor, letting it burn down his gullet as he studied the photograph once more. The rain drummed on outside—different from that night, but just as relentless. No matter how many times he replayed the events in his mind like an old movie reel, they never went any further than that one fateful moment before flickering away into darkness.

A knock at his office door shattered the spell and pulled him back to the present—the kind of abruptness only served by strangers. He raised an eyebrow, wondering who would disturb a Tuesday evening alone time on this forsaken night when all he sought was solitude. The man who answered wasn’t one for small talk or idle chatter; Jack’s gut recognized it as a prelude to another miserable chapter in his life.

“Come in,” slurred just short of hostility, an uninvited guest. Slade turned, watching the door swing shut behind a man whose face seemed crafted from equal parts exhaustion and desperation—a ruddy complexion, greased-back hair that spoke of better days—and settled into a worn armchair.

“I’m Frank Jenkins.” His voice cracked and rasped like weathered leather, but Jack recognized nothing else in those eyes to suggest familiarity from the force or civilian life. “Please—”

“Make it quick,” he cut him off, not quite rude enough for disdain yet lacking the warmth needed to foster genuine conversation; just another human thrown into his orbit.

“I’m sorry.” Frank Jenkins shook his head and pulled a crumpled photograph from an inner breast pocket, offering it like an apology. A silver locket dangled beside it on its chain—the type of trinket little girls adored. “This is my daughter Sarah—Sarah Marie.”

Slade felt a pang in his chest as he took both objects in a heavy hand. She’d been thirteen then, a fragile doll of light brown hair and perpetual smile. His own flesh and blood had resembled her once; that was no accident. A memory flickered—the way she used to hug him tight, burying her face into his chest without fail when the rain lashed down outside, an act both tender and suffocating in its ferocity.

In this city, each loss carved its own melody into Jack’s heart. He’d heard it before; it was a refrain he knew all too well. “When did you notice she was missing?” Slade asked, his voice firm but not unkind.

“Three nights ago,” Frank replied, voice still heavy with emotion that didn’t belong here among these dingy confines where lives crumbled and men paid for their flaws. Jack’s gut twisted; not the first time such a scenario had played out in his office.

“I’m guessing three weeks is right?” The man nodded through his own tears as he looked away, eyes streaming on one side of a battered face that didn’t belong to him or the world he’d left behind. “She never came home from her job. Said she was working late at this dingy movie theater near the city limits—the very last place I spoke to her.” Frank’s breath hitched and for an instant Jack thought it might be all he could do not exhale too in response.

“And you haven’t filed a report?” Slade asked, though not genuinely surprised. They never came knocking when they needed help; only when hope had fled and desperation took over where the law fell short.

Jenkins nodded again, eyes watering heavily now as an unspoken tale swirled behind them—a story of loss, of shattered sleepless nights he’d lived a thousand days since Sarah’s vanishing. Slade recognized that despair—this man had gone from worried to consumed; there was no coming back from such precipice, not truly.

“I need you to find her.” The rain drummed on outside like a death knell as they shared an uncomfortable silence that wasn’t quite one between strangers yet held the weight of years. “Why me?” The question hung in air between them while raindrops tracked down Jack’s window pane like small silver snakes—each a single bead of water shedding from a world full of such mysteries he’d never solved.

“I can pay, Mr Slade.” Frank Jenkins’ voice cracked with desperation as he rummaged through his coat lapels before dropping into an opposing chair as if finding it a relief.

“Jack,” he corrected before forcing his focus back to the man; the name seemed lost on him. “I’ll take it.” It was better than begging for hope—a futile endeavor when all you wanted in return were nightmares and bills paid by daybreak. They shook hands without enthusiasm.

Jenkins slid a thick envelope across worn wood, an offer of twenty percent more substantial than he deserved; Jack tucked it away before accepting the photographs—Sarah Marie Jenkins: 17 years old, eyes that still sparkled under light, full lips. Slade’s own daughter had left him at nineteen for college and life outside the rain-soaked city limits but there was something about this girl that stirred in his chest.

The man didn’t waste words as he recounted her last known whereabouts or when she’d be expected home. “I’ve been driving around these three weeks,” Frank Jenkins whispered, voice a keening wail. Jack could relate; nights of wandering the very same streets Sarah took on foot to work—the movie theater.

“You lost something else in that car.” It was almost an accusation but didn’t sound like it coming from those haunted eyes. A photograph of a child clutched between his shaking hands offered insight—Sarah Marie’s school picture, hair styled the way she preferred; a ponytail and bangs framing features as sweetly innocent as her mother once claimed.

The rain intensified in its assault on the building, rattling windows they both avoided meeting eyes for now. “Tell me everything.” And with that simple command Jack slid into his worn armchair once more, letting the familiar contours guide him back through grief’s labyrinthine passages—the ones he’d navigated all too recently—though this time it wasn’t rain outside but heartbreak.

He took up a notebook as Frank Jenkins spoke in fits and starts of her disappearance: a late shift at Starlight Theater; Sarah never calling home or meeting friends for their ritual coffee date the next morning, not packing an overnight bag that had become a habit since high school. Slade knew this story—knew the questions they’d soon face, those long shots into the night as he canvassed what little leads existed.

The rain lashed down with renewed ferocity and Frank’s words fell away for one moment. He felt it—the familiar sting of memories in the back of his throat—a mix of grief and whiskey. Hours blurred together, each drop merging with the last to create a relentless beat that pounded against Jack’s eardrums.

Sarah Marie Jenkins had been gone twenty-five days that morning—three weeks ago tonight, exactly like now. Slade closed the notebook as a name scratched itself across his mind like acid on the cheap paper: Frank. The man’s eyes pleaded silently to find her as Jack reached for another swig of whiskey while the rain kept pouring outside; it was either drown in the past or dive back into the abyss, and right now that glass felt lighter by far.

“I’ll need all your dates.” He scribbled with a pen heavy from neglect. “I can pay,” he repeated to himself, an act of futility as they were paid for—money in this city was blood spilled on pavement, and his office the repository of both.

Rain laced through guttering streetlights like veins of neon steel outside. Sarah Marie Jenkins had been seventeen then; it’s not that much older than nineteen when he last held her close... The rain drummed louder still as Jack Slade took up a case long overdue from this city and his heart—a habit neither sought to break, yet here they both were.

“I see.” A worn briefcase clutched by his feet contained little else but the clothes on a warm summer’s evening—Sarah Marie’s school picture and her name spelled out in hasty script. Frank Jenkins passed it to Jack Slade as they both felt time stretching longer than the heavy rain outside—a silent conversation about unspoken vows made between them—the promise of hope in this deluge.

“You can pay?” Frank had offered, eyes red-rimmed but not quite pleading as he asked for something that seemed a farce—a man begging to breathe again. Slade tucked away his notebook and leaned forward just enough so the rain shadowed one side of his face like a cruel kiss from an old flame.

“Get out,” he said, motioning towards the door. “You got somewhere you didn’t think she’d be?” They exchanged phone numbers—Frank’s trembling hand, Jack’s steady—and the investigator stood.

The man lingered as Slade scribbled down his number; rain-soaked streets called out like an old lover’s plea through the cracked walls of Jack’s office—each drop insistent, a symphony to which he’d long been accustomed. He could smell smoke, a precursor that had become both familiarity and dread—a habit forged by nightfall walks when darkness fell, the world a different shade at his daughter Sarah Marie Jenkins’ height.

“Call me when you’re ready,” Frank said finally, voice barely above a whisper as he turned to face out into the downpour before turning up the collar of his trench and walking back through a city where rain was an unwelcome guest, if not outright foe. “Frank,” Slade called after him—the only name he knew for now—and when it returned there’d be two missing girls, another life, or both.

A door closed behind him; the detective sipped, eyes still lost in thoughts of how they were connected—more than coincidence given these rain-soaked streets. His cell buzzed with a promise—a mystery about to unfurl like this relentless rain, dripping outside and inside his skull alike—the past, threatening to drown him once more.

The number displayed as he reached into an interior pocket. On the screen it read “Jenkins.” He dialed before lifting eyes that’d seen better times. A female voice on the other end—a breathy hello—then, finally: “I’m listening.”

“Listen close,” she said, her voice quivering—just as the night was now, clinging onto hope; Jack Slade understood them both quite well—a shared language of fear. They talked through the night as rain fell in sheets—Sarah Jenkins’ life unraveling like his own, the sound of them sharing what little Frank recalled—a story he knew to heart already though it was new, just as those drops had been for so long now; Jack’s eyes felt watered from more than the torrential downpour.

He jotted details by dim light—the first drop—Sarah Marie Jenkins.
